## Olchani Project

`olchani-project`  **— new project**

**Excerpt**

Olchani means tree in the Maa language. It began with one nursery in Amboseli in 2021 and has become a way of life: communities growing indigenous trees, and earning a dignified living doing it.

**Content**

*Olchani* is the Maa word for tree, and the project is what the name suggests — growing indigenous trees across the Amboseli ecosystem. Not as a planting day, but as work that people are paid for, own, and keep doing.

It started in **May 2021** with a location and a promise. **Dr Winnie Kiiru** offered her place in the Amboseli area to host the first tree nursery, and **Jim Nyamu** agreed to help care for the seedlings. Four trees went into the ground to inaugurate it, and the invitation was open to anyone who wanted to help reforest the ecosystem — for the elephants and giraffe that live in it, and for the clean air of the people who live there too.

From that beginning the work became communal. Nurseries were established with youth at the Kenya Wildlife Service’s Amboseli headquarters and at Oldoinyo Oibor Primary School, and then in the communities themselves: the Osoit nursery — *Intomonok Osoit*, built for ten thousand seedlings — and later the Ildepen and Tembo nurseries. Local women, the *mamas*, raise the seedlings and earn a real income for it. Decisions are made in *barazas*, the community meetings where everyone with a stake in the land has a voice.

The seedlings are planted out into restoration sites, chief among them the **Orregie Enclosure** inside Amboseli National Park — envisaged as a small forest of five to seven thousand indigenous trees, helping to hold a wildlife corridor open. On National Tree Planting Day the community put four hundred seedlings into the ground there in a single day. Beyond the restoration sites, the project carries tree-growing into households, churches and schools, putting seedlings directly into families’ hands.

The partnership with **ifaw** has grown alongside it; when ifaw marked twenty-five years of conservation work in East Africa, its team planted trees raised in these community nurseries. And the thinking has extended beyond trees: Olchani’s second phase added grass and fodder, and became **Ubuntu Hay**, the community hay bank at Lemongo.

The foundation puts it in its own words: restoration is more than planting trees — mamas leading community nurseries and earning dignified incomes, youth growing trees while growing their purpose, communities healing land while healing livelihoods.

## The Elephant Den

`the-elephant-den`  **— new project**

**Excerpt**

A summit where young people from five Kenyan counties pitched real businesses for a grant to start them — conservation’s next generation, backed with capital rather than advice alone.

**Content**

Young people who live alongside wildlife need a living that does not come at wildlife’s expense. **The Elephant Den** — TED — was the foundation’s answer: not a workshop, but a competition with money at the end of it.

In March 2022 the foundation and Stand Up Shout Out brought five groups of young Kenyans together at the SUSO headquarters, where they camped for the summit. They came from Nakuru, Amboseli, Taita Taveta, Embu and Nairobi, and each group presented a commercial business built for the place they actually live, competing for a grant to start it. A panel of four heard them out — Peter Karanja and Annabella Francescon for the foundation, Peter Moll and Poebe for SUSO — and gave advice aimed at turning the ideas into working enterprises. The proposals were, in the foundation’s words, impressive and well thought out for the areas they come from.

It did not end at the pitch. The following month the foundation handed four acres of land near Embu town to SUSO’s chief executive Peter Moll and the Embu County Director, to be set up as a tree nursery and a base for other agricultural work — a commercial venture for young people that is also good for the land it sits on.

The foundation described TED at the time as a new chapter: empowering youth into the future with commercial enterprises for their own livelihoods. The thinking behind it — that conservation holds when the people nearest to wildlife have something to gain from it — runs through everything the foundation has built since.

## Walk for Elephants with Jim Nyamu

`walk-for-elephants-with-jim-nyamu`

**Excerpt**

Since Jim Nyamu's first walk in 2013, the foundation has walked every one of them with him, carrying the message that ivory belongs to elephants across Kenya and far beyond it.

**Content**

Jim Nyamu walks. Since 2013, when he set out from Mombasa and covered 1,500 kilometres to put the plight of the elephant in front of the people who live alongside them, he has crossed Kenya and much of East Africa on foot under one banner: *Ivory Belongs to Elephants*. The foundation took the first steps of that first walk with him, and has joined every walk since.

What that support looks like is practical. The team flags Jim off at the start, walks stretches of the route beside him, stops with him to talk to communities along the way about their part in protecting elephants, and is waiting at the finish. In August 2021 the foundation walked the last stretch into Amboseli so that Jim arrived for World Elephant Day on the twelfth. That November, as he set out through the Great Rift Valley, the walk carried 10,000 trees to be planted along the route — 3,500 of them seedlings from the foundation.

The walks have grown longer. In July 2023 the foundation was at the flag-off of Jim's East and Central Africa walk through Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo, and travelled to the Malaba border to see the team across. In October 2024 the Horn of Africa Elephant Campaign Walk ended on its eighty-sixth day with the ceremonial handover of the Kenyan flag at the foundation, where Managing Director Peter Karanja and the team welcomed the walkers home. In April 2022, when Jim received the Presidential Order of the Grand Warrior, the award ceremony was held in the foundation's own garden.

## Sponsoring exams

`sponsoring-exams`

**Excerpt**

Standing behind students through the examinations that decide where they go next — and staying in their lives long enough to see the results come in.

**Content**

In Kenya a national examination decides a child’s next school, and with it a great deal of what follows. The foundation backs students through that moment: the years of schooling before it, the sitting of the examinations themselves, and the place the results earn.

The support is personal rather than administrative. The foundation has followed individual students through the Stand Up Shout Out network for years — among the earliest, Brenda and Faith, whom the team took to the cinema in Nairobi in 2019 with their SUSO mentor Yvonne to mark how well they were doing at school. Staying close enough to know how a child is doing is what makes it possible to step in at the point where money, not ability, would otherwise decide the outcome.

Where strong results open a door, the foundation’s scholarship programme carries the student through it.

## Scholarships

`scholarships`  **— new project**

**Excerpt**

Full secondary-school scholarships for young people whose examination results have earned them a place they could not otherwise take up, awarded with Stand Up Shout Out.

**Content**

A strong national examination result opens the door to a good secondary school. The cost of that school closes it again for families who cannot pay. The foundation’s scholarship programme exists for exactly that gap.

The scholarships are awarded together with Stand Up Shout Out, to young people already being mentored through SUSO’s programmes, and they are full ones — decided on the strength of KCPE results, of talent, and of what the student has already put back into their community. They are awarded for who a young person is becoming, not only for a mark on a paper.

In 2022 three young women from Kibra and Dagoretti, all of them from SUSO’s *Own Your Crown* programme for women and girls’ leadership, won full scholarships on their KCPE results and enrolled in excellent Kenyan high schools: Sarah, Hope and Fantasia. They reported for Form 1 that May.

The foundation and SUSO describe what the scholarships carry as more than an education — character and leadership development, and a commitment to walk with each young leader the whole way. The aim is a strong, resilient community, built by people who were raised inside it and chose to stay and lift it.

## Providing water to wildlife in Kenya's national parks

`providing-water-to-wildlife`

**Excerpt**

When the dry season empties the waterholes, the foundation hires bowsers and fills them — 88,000 litres into Tsavo East on one World Elephant Day alone.

**Content**

Kenya's dry seasons decide which animals live. Waterholes shrink to cracked mud, troughs run empty, and wildlife walks further and further for a drink, often into conflict with people. Where a park cannot hold water through a drought, the foundation trucks it in.

On World Elephant Day in August 2020 the foundation hired seven bowsers carrying 88,000 litres into Tsavo East, where Kenya's largest elephant population lives, and used them to fill three waterholes, a mud pit and three water troughs. Thirty young people from Stand Up Shout Out travelled from Nairobi to Voi to do the work, on a day that also fell on Youth Day, with the Kenya Wildlife Service providing collaboration and security. In September 2021 the foundation returned to Tsavo with eight bowsers and 96,000 litres, filling troughs and a waterhole that had dried out completely.

The hardest test came with the drought of early 2023, in Nairobi National Park. There the foundation worked inside a coalition — alongside Friends of Nairobi National Park, BAPS Charity, the PKF Foundation and SUSO, with the Kenya Wildlife Service facilitating — to keep water and fodder going into the park for two months. One March delivery alone brought 77,000 litres of water and 450 bales of hay, and the final run filled a desilted dam just before the rains arrived. Across the two months that coalition — Friends of Nairobi National Park, BAPS Charity, the PKF Foundation, Stand Up Shout Out, the foundation and a long list of individual donors — delivered **1.2 million litres of water, 2,064 bales of hay and 203 bales of lucerne** into the park. The figure belongs to all of them together; the foundation was one part of it.

## Planting mangroves at the coast

`planting-mangroves-at-the-coast`  **— written, slug changed**

**Excerpt**

Mangroves are the only trees on earth built to live between land and sea. At Kenya's coast the foundation backs Stand Up Shout Out's Adopt A Creek programme, which has put tens of thousands of them back into the mud.

**Content**

Mangroves grow where the tide meets the shore, rooted in salt and mud, and they hold the coast together: they break the force of waves before those waves reach a village, they trap the sediment that would otherwise wash the shoreline away, they store several times more carbon than most forests on land, and their tangled roots are the nursery for much of the fish the coast lives on. Kenya has been losing them.

The planting is run by **Stand Up Shout Out**, the youth organisation the foundation has worked alongside since the beginning, through its *Adopt A Creek* programme, and the foundation supports it. The work is concentrated in the creeks around Mombasa — **Jomvu Kuu** and **Tudor Creek** — where, from 2021 onward, more than ten thousand mangrove seedlings were planted, chiefly *Rhizophora mucronata* and *Avicennia marina*, the species that do best in those waters. The Jomvu Kuu work is a partnership with the Bidii Creek Community Conservation group.

Planting is only half of it; the seedlings have to be raised first. The programme runs coastal nurseries — five thousand mangroves nursed at Jomvu Kuu and Tudor Creek, and one nursery grown to more than ten thousand seedlings — and has expanded along the coastline from Kwale to Kilifi. On **Wasini Island** on the south coast it was extended with Shimoni Turtle Watch and the Wasini Women Group, who set up a village nursery of just over a thousand *Ceriops tagal* seedlings.

What makes it hold is who does it. The nurseries belong to the women’s groups and community conservation groups that run them, and the young people who plant the seedlings are from the villages the mangroves protect. Improved livelihoods, powered by conservation — hands in the mud, on the coast they belong to.

## Gifting boots to game rangers

`gifting-boots-to-game-rangers`  **— seven-year poaching record in**

**Excerpt**

Rangers walk for a living. At a rhino ceremony with KWS at Lake Nakuru, the foundation gave them boots — and kept asking what else they needed.

**Content**

A ranger's job is walking. Patrols cover long distances over rough, wet, thorned ground, day after day, and boots wear out faster than they are replaced. In September 2020, at a rhino ceremony held with the Kenya Wildlife Service at Lake Nakuru National Park, the foundation and the Stand Up Shout Out team handed boots to the rangers there — both to make the work possible and to thank them for keeping the park's rhinos, and all its wildlife, safe.

The gesture came out of time spent alongside them. Through the foundation's many days of clean-up work in Nairobi National Park, rangers led the volunteers, kept them safe and worked beside them, while their colleagues stayed out on the patrols that protect the park's rhinos — a watch that has kept the sanctuary free of rhino poaching for over seven years. What rangers need is best learned from rangers, and the foundation has kept asking.

The answers have shaped everything since. In June 2022, twenty Kenya Wildlife Service rangers were honoured at Nairobi National Park and given twenty powerful rechargeable torches — equipment they had specifically asked for to strengthen their night operations. The same instinct, followed further, became the foundation's Dignity Housing Project.

A working trip to one of Kenya's great lake parks, spent on the ground with the Stand Up Shout Out youth team and the rangers who look after it.

## Sustaining community livelihoods during COVID-19

`sustaining-community-livelihoods-during-covid-19`

**Excerpt**

When the pandemic closed Kenya's schools and emptied its hotels, the foundation ran a weekly distribution into Kibra — 500 families, 3,000 people, week after week.

**Content**

COVID-19 hit hardest where people earn a daily wage. In Kibra, one of Nairobi's largest informal settlements, work disappeared almost overnight, and with schools closed the children lost their classrooms too. In April 2020 the foundation began funding a weekly distribution of food and books there, run with Maniago Safaris and the Stand Up Shout Out network led by Peter Moll, reaching 500 families — around 3,000 people.

It was run carefully. Fresh vegetables and fruit were sourced directly from nearby farms; a screening system controlled entry to the distribution centre; masks were compulsory and special overalls were made to keep the distributors safe. Beyond the food, the team sprayed matatus and boda bodas to keep the vehicles hygienic, and set up large buckets of water with taps, kept refilled, so that people passing by could wash their hands.

Education ran alongside it. The fourth distribution focused on schooling: 431 children received education packages designed by their own teachers, so they could study at home and hand work back to be marked. There were no computers involved — every package was assembled and every book carried by hand, with teachers returning to closed schools to collect and return the children's work.

The work spread beyond Kibra. Food was driven out to communities near the Maasai Mara whose livelihoods had collapsed with tourism. By July 2020 the programme had reached its tenth distribution and thousands of recipients. That September it turned from relief to repair: with the SUSO team, the foundation backed a Community Challenge that cleaned, painted and built pathways at the Phoenix Library and Three Bells Primary School, and began a green space beside the library that the young people named Little Karura.

## Eradicating the invasive Parthenium weed

`eradicating-the-invasive-parthenium-weed`  **— slug typo fixed**

**Excerpt**

Parthenium chokes the grasslands of Nairobi National Park. The foundation put 130 people to work pulling it out, and paid them, in a year when work had vanished.

**Content**

Parthenium is an invasive weed that spreads fast through open grassland, crowding out the grasses that grazing wildlife depends on. It had begun to take hold in Nairobi National Park, and the only reliable way to remove it is by hand.

In June 2020 the foundation sponsored an operation to do exactly that. A hundred and thirty people — the Maniago team, young volunteers from Stand Up Shout Out and residents of the Kibera settlement — went into the park to uproot the weed, protected throughout by Kenya Wildlife Service rangers, and the exercise was repeated twice a week over the following months.

It did two jobs at once. The work paid a small wage at a moment when the pandemic had wiped out livelihoods, and it took people into a park on their own doorstep that most of them had never been inside. They saw lions, rhino, giraffe and antelope in the course of a working day. Kenyan guides who had lost their own work joined the effort too, among them four of Maniago's own.

The removals have continued as a partnership. On World Environment Day in June 2022, SUSO, Maniago Safaris and the Kenya Wildlife Service cleared the murram pits between points 8 and 9 in the park, pulling out 611 kilogrammes of parthenium with more than eighty participants on the day.

## Tree planting at Nairobi National Park

`tree-planting-at-the-nairobi-national-park`

**Excerpt**

Five hundred trees planted in the park on the city's doorstep, in holes the young volunteers dug themselves.

**Content**

Nairobi is the only capital city in the world with a national park inside its limits, and keeping that park alive is a civic as much as a conservation task. In October 2021 the foundation and the youth of Stand Up Shout Out spent days in the park on the hard physical part of tree planting — digging the holes — before an event that put 500 trees into the ground. The project was designed by Olga Levari and Trish Heather Hayes, two women who have long made the park their business.

It was part of something larger. That May the foundation had joined a national greening campaign with SUSO, the Kenya Forest Service and the National Youth Council, which planted 30,000 seedlings across more than ten Kenyan towns and cities on a single day and aimed at 400,000 seedlings before the end of June 2021.

Planting is only the beginning of a tree's life, and the pattern set here — young people growing, planting and then tending what they put in the ground — became the way the foundation works with trees everywhere it works.

## Dignity Housing for wildlife rangers

`dignity-housing-for-wildife-rangers`  **— timeline corrected; seven-year poaching record and $350,000 in**

**Excerpt**

Rangers spend weeks at a time living where the wildlife they protect lives, and for years many did so in tents with no beds, kitchen or bathroom. Since 2021 Dignity Housing has been building them proper homes: self-contained units with beds, a kitchenette, a shower and flush toilet, solar power and water tanks, in Nairobi National Park, Amboseli and Samburu.

**Content**

Rangers spend weeks at a time living where the wildlife they protect lives. For years, many of them did so in basic tents and worn-out shelters, in the rhino sanctuary of Nairobi National Park and beyond.

The project began in **March 2021**, with a warden’s idea. Joseph Dadacha, Senior Warden of Nairobi National Park, wanted to replace the small tents — no beds, no bathroom, no kitchen — that housed the rhino rangers living inside the park’s rhino sanctuary. Maniago Safaris, the foundation and Stand Up Shout Out designed and built the first two self-contained container homes together and moved them into the first two rhino blocks, then fitted water tanks, plumbing and solar power so that the rangers had running water, warm showers and electricity. There are twenty-five rhino blocks in the park, and the foundation set out to build a home for every one of them: twenty-five fully fitted homes at US$14,000 each, a programme of US$350,000.

The first two homes were in place by April 2021, a bio-digester dug in beside them and bushes planted around them as camouflage. The third and fourth followed that August. In September 2021 the foundation brought Martin Meyer and prospective donors into the park to see them, and Senior Warden Dadacha explained what these particular rangers do: through their determination, their training and sheer commitment, **not one rhino had been poached in Nairobi National Park in over seven years**, while the population went on growing with new calves.

The Luigi Footprints Foundation developed **Dignity Housing** to give these rangers self-contained container homes. Each home has three beds, a small kitchenette, a shower and flush toilet, solar power for lighting and hot water, and large water tanks. The foundation has now built these homes across three landscapes: Amboseli, Nairobi National Park and Samburu National Reserve. Around the homes, the foundation and the rangers plant trees together.

The fifth house was launched there in July 2025, when the Kenya Wildlife Service read a message of appreciation for the partnership between KWS, Maniago Safaris, the foundation and Stand Up Shout Out, and the sixth was handed over to the Rhino Rangers in October 2025.

In October 2025 the work crossed to Samburu National Reserve. After a meeting by the Ewaso Nyiro River between the foundation's Peter Karanja, Honorary Warden Annabella Francescon and Reserve Director Tom Lesarge, ground was broken in November with Tom Lesarge and contractor Benson Omolo, and construction ran through December. The units were donated by trustee Annabella Francescon.

In early 2026 the Governor of Samburu County, H.E. Jonathan Lati Lelelit, officially opened the Samburu Dignity Housing: eight units, a kitchen and washrooms at a bridge crossing that had never had a permanent ranger base. The security point can now be manned, and rangers can respond faster. On World Ranger Day 2026 the foundation returned to plant trees around the homes.

Each fully fitted home costs US$14,000, and twenty-five of them come to US$350,000. If a whole unit is funded, a plaque with your name or the name of your organisation goes onto the home.
